# Medicines that calm the immune system: for everyone

This part explains what to expect while you take medicine that calms your immune system. Use the checklists to keep track of your blood tests and vaccines.

C1 covers things everyone on these medicines should know. C2 covers your specific medicine. You only need to read the sections for the medicines you take.

### Why these medicines need extra care

In your condition, the immune system attacks your own nerves, muscles, or brain. These medicines calm the immune system down. That protects your body, but there are tradeoffs:

- Your body fights some infections less well.

- Some vaccines work less well, and some vaccines are not safe to get.

- Some medicines need regular blood tests. These tests catch side effects early, often before you feel anything.

Following the steps below keeps these risks low.

### Checklist 1: Blood tests before you start

Some infections, like tuberculosis (TB) and hepatitis B, can hide in the body for years without symptoms. They can wake up when the immune system is calmed down. We check for them first so we can treat them safely.

☐ CMP (kidney, liver, blood sugar, and salts)

### If you take prednisone:

- **Shingrix and COVID vaccines are safe at any prednisone dose.** They do not contain live virus, so they cannot give you shingles or COVID.

- **At 20 mg a day or more, vaccines may work less well.** Prednisone can weaken your body's response to a vaccine. If your dose will be lower soon, we may wait until it is below 20 mg so you get better protection. If you will be on a high dose for a long time, or COVID is spreading, we may give them now. Ask us what is best for you.

- **Flu shot:** get it every fall on time, at any dose.

- **Required vaccines** (meningitis and hepatitis B) are given on schedule at any prednisone dose.

**Live vaccines.** Avoid these unless we tell you they are safe:

- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)

- Chickenpox (Varivax), **except** before starting certain MS medicines (fingolimod, Mayzent, Mavenclad) if you are not immune. See your medicine section.

- Nasal spray flu vaccine (FluMist)

- Yellow fever

Always ask us before getting travel vaccines.

**Vaccines during pregnancy:** flu, COVID, and other routine pregnancy vaccines are usually recommended. Ask your obstetrician. **If you took an immune medicine during pregnancy** (such as rituximab, Ocrevus, Kesimpta, Uplizna, or Soliris), tell your baby's doctor. Your baby may need to wait before getting **live** vaccines (such as the rotavirus vaccine).

**Other vaccines** (pneumonia, RSV, tetanus, HPV, and others): ask your primary care doctor to keep you up to date. Show them this sheet.

### About your results:

- We review every result. If a result means something needs to change, we will contact you.

- Some results are expected to change, and that does not mean something is wrong. For example, lymphocytes (a type of white blood cell) drop on many MS medicines, and IgG (antibody level) drops on FcRn medicines. This is often a sign the medicine is working.

- If you get blood drawn at an outside lab, make sure the results are sent to us.

### Preventing infections

- Wash your hands often. Stay away from people who are sick when you can.

- Tell every doctor, dentist, and urgent care clinic that you take immune-suppressing medicine.

- **Fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher:** call us the same day.

- **If you have an infection:** call us before your next dose. We may pause your medicine until you are better.

- **Never stop prednisone suddenly**, even when you are sick.

### Go to the emergency room or call 911 for:

- Fever with a stiff neck, bad headache, confusion, or a new rash (this is very important if you take Ultomiris, Soliris, or Zilbrysq)

- Trouble breathing, swallowing, or speaking (myasthenia gravis)

- Swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat

- Chest pain, sudden weakness on one side, or sudden vision loss

- Fever with shaking chills, or feeling very ill

### If you have NMOSD or MOGAD, call us the same day (or go to the ER if severe) for signs of a possible attack:

- New vision loss, blurry or dim vision, or eye pain when moving the eye

- New numbness, tingling, or weakness in your legs, arms, or body

- New trouble controlling your bladder or bowels

- Hiccups or vomiting that will not stop

- New double vision or trouble with balance

Attacks are treated best when treated early. Do not wait for your next visit.

### Protecting your skin and screening for cancer

- Use sunscreen (SPF 30 or higher) and wear a hat outdoors.

- Get a skin check every year, or as directed in your medicine section.

- Keep up with routine cancer screenings, such as mammograms, Pap tests, and colon cancer screening.

### Pregnancy and family planning

- Many immune medicines can harm a pregnancy. Some medicines also matter for men who want to father a child (for example, Mavenclad and cyclophosphamide).

- **Talk to us before you start trying for a pregnancy.** Some medicines must be stopped weeks to months ahead. See the **"stop ahead of time" table in A2.**

- **Some immune medicines are generally OK in pregnancy** (such as prednisone, azathioprine, IVIG, and Copaxone), so many people can stay protected during pregnancy.

- **Your disease can flare after delivery**, especially MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, and myasthenia. We will plan your treatment for after the baby is born, including breastfeeding.

- If you become pregnant, **do not stop your medicine on your own.** Call us the same day.

- More information: **MotherToBaby** at https://mothertobaby.org or 1-866-626-6847.

### Other medicines and supplements

- Check with us or your pharmacist before starting any new medicine. This includes antibiotics, gout medicine, over-the-counter pain relievers, and supplements.

- Use one pharmacy if you can, so they can check for interactions.

- Keep an up-to-date medicine list in your wallet or phone.

- **Taking phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, or carbamazepine?** These can make prednisone and some other medicines work less well. See **A3, Alert 2**.
